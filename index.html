A sequel to “The Housing Market is NUTS”, tested on Australia and New Zealand

Housing Effort Down Under

“Employment concentration is the elephant in the house – the structural force that no amount of tinkering with mortgage rates, construction permits, or tourist regulations will fully overcome.”

Conclusion of The Housing Market is NUTS

That European study ends on a strong claim. Across 312 regions in Portugal, Spain, France, Italy and the Netherlands, housing costs the most months of wage where jobs are densest. Tourism only modulates that pattern. Capitals keep gaining share of national employment, and every return-to-office memo feeds the same corner.

If that is how cities work, it should hold outside Europe. Australia and New Zealand make a hard test. They have some of the least affordable housing in the OECD, very different urban systems (two giant capitals in Australia, one dominant city in NZ), and better regional data than Europe. I re-ran the method on 50 Australian labour-market regions and 57 New Zealand districts, mapped the same variables, and extended it back to 1986.

The rest of this page follows the original's structure: a review of the European piece, the data, maps of where jobs and housing costs are, the density fits, why New Zealand differs, tourism, the mortgage cliff, concentration, and forty years of change.

Part one

Review of the original

“The Housing Market is NUTS” builds a regional affordability metric for 312 NUTS-3 regions in Portugal, Spain, France, Italy and the Netherlands. The metric is months of gross wage per square metre. It fits that against job density and tourism, simulates mortgages, and tracks employment concentration since 2000. Its argument is that density, not tourism, is the structural driver, and that re-concentration of jobs (return-to-office, capital-centric investment) keeps feeding the problem.

What it does well

  • The price-to-local-wage ratio is the right unit. It removes currency, inflation and wage-level differences, which the Eurostat cost-burden indicator cannot.
  • The critique of EU-SILC is correct. Counting mortgage interest but not principal makes cross-country burden rankings depend on tenure mix.
  • It is transparent: raw inputs, derived files, fallback flags and the Paris merge are all documented.
  • It checks the result against an external source (OECD HC1.2 median mortgage burden), and the country ordering agrees.
  • It treats tourism as a second variable instead of just deleting tourist regions.

Where it overreaches

  • Causality. The parsimony argument says a confounder would need to produce “a specific logarithmic form”. A log-linear shape is the generic outcome of almost any process that scales with city size, and amenity, land-use rules and geography all rise with density.
  • Composition bias. Dense regions sell smaller flats, and small units carry higher prices per m². Part of the density gradient in a per-m² metric is a dwelling-mix effect.
  • Outlier-cleaned R². Dropping residual outliers raises R² by construction. The five-country fit is 0.27 before cleaning, and the headline 0.64 is cleaned.
  • No supply variable. Dwellings per worker or building approvals would test the mechanism directly. Without one, “density causes effort” is a hypothesis.
  • Policy leap. Concentration rising alongside unaffordability does not show that decentralisation would fix it. People may simply be moving to where productivity is.

None of this sinks the piece. Its descriptive findings are solid, and its central claim makes a testable prediction: wherever jobs are dense, effort should be high. Australia and New Zealand are a good out-of-sample test. They have very different urban systems, much better regional data, and housing crises at least as severe.

Part two

The data crisis is a European problem

The original spends a whole section on how hard it was to assemble the data. In Australia the equivalent took one government workbook. The ABS Data by Region release publishes, for every one of 88 SA4 regions, median established-house and attached-dwelling sale prices (CoreLogic/Cotality transfers), median employee income from tax returns, employee jobs by industry, population and land area. Census 2021 adds employment by place of work. New Zealand needs three sources: HUD's sale prices from the District Valuation Roll, Stats NZ business demography for employees by district and industry, and MBIE's regional dataset for LEED earnings and commercial guest nights.

What neither country publishes is price per square metre. So the effort metric here is years of median annual wage needed to buy the median dwelling. It is the same ratio idea at dwelling scale. It is not directly comparable with the original's months per m², because the typical Australian or NZ dwelling is far larger than 50 m².

Geography. Australian SA4s slice Sydney into 15 pieces, so the CBD unit holds 9,357 jobs/km² while its workers live elsewhere. This is the Paris problem the original solved by merging. Here, each capital's SA4s are merged into its Greater Capital City area, giving 8 capitals plus 42 regional SA4s. In NZ, cities drawn tight around their urban cores are merged with their commuter districts (Wellington with the Hutts and Porirua, Christchurch with Selwyn and Waimakariri, Hamilton, Tauranga, Napier–Hastings). All fits were also run on the unmerged units, and the conclusions hold.
Part three

Where the jobs are, and where housing costs the most

The original opens its regional analysis with maps of job density, then maps of effort. These are the same maps for Australia and New Zealand. As in the original, the diverging colours are centred on the middle of the range. Blue means sparser or more affordable, red means denser or less affordable. Hover or tap a region for its value. The region tables in the notebook have every number. Australia is mapped at SA4 level (88 regions, with insets for four capitals), so the capitals appear split into their parts here, unlike in the fits.

In Australia the two maps line up along the east coast. Job density is red only in the capital cores, Newcastle, Illawarra, Geelong and the Gold and Sunshine Coasts. Effort is red in the same belt, from Sydney and its coast north through Richmond–Tweed to the Sunshine Coast, and in Melbourne's inner east and south. The interior and the mining regions of Western Australia and Queensland are blue. They are sparse, but high mining wages also keep effort low. Inside Sydney, effort peaks in the Eastern Suburbs and North Shore rather than the CBD, because wages here are measured where people live.

The original maps employment change to show capitals pulling away. Australia shows the reverse inside its cities. Jobs held by residents grew fastest on the outer suburban fringe: Melbourne West +35%, Ipswich +34%, Perth North East +34%, Logan +33% and Blacktown +32%. They grew slowest in inner Sydney (Eastern Suburbs +4%, City and Inner South +6%). Workers are moving outward, to where housing is cheaper, and commuting in.

This series counts jobs by where the worker lives, so it measures where workers settle rather than where offices are.

New Zealand's two maps do not line up. Density is red only in the cities: Auckland, Hamilton, Tauranga, Napier, Palmerston North, Wellington, Porirua and Lower Hutt, Christchurch, Nelson and Invercargill. Effort is red in Auckland, but just as red in Thames-Coromandel, the Western Bay of Plenty, Tasman, Central Otago and Queenstown-Lakes, none of which are dense. Christchurch and Dunedin, both real job centres, are grey or blue.

Growth is the better fit. Employment grew fastest in Queenstown-Lakes (+48%), Selwyn (+38%), Porirua (+37%), Upper Hutt and Waikato (+31%), against +16% nationally, and shrank in Waitomo, Kaikōura, Tararua and Clutha. Over twenty years, effort rose most in the belt around Auckland and Hamilton (Waikato +6.9 years, Kaipara, Waipā, Matamata-Piako) and in lifestyle districts. It rose least in Wellington, Nelson, Tasman, Buller and Kaikōura. Parts four and five test this formally.

Part four

Density and effort

Each dot is a region. The x-axis is jobs per km² on a log scale, the y-axis is years of median wage per median dwelling, and the dashed line is the log-linear fit the original uses. Ringed dots are the regions flagged as outliers by the same IQR rule on fit residuals.

In Australia the original's pattern reappears. Each tenfold rise in job density adds about 2 years of wage to the median dwelling. The outliers are the same kind the original found: Algarve-style coastal regions where retirees, remote workers and holiday buyers bid up prices against low local wages. Sunshine Coast and Gold Coast sit just inside the fences.

New Zealand breaks the pattern. Auckland is expensive, but so is almost everywhere with a lake, a beach or a ski field. Christchurch, Hamilton and Dunedin, which are real employment centres, sit mid-table.

Part five

Why New Zealand breaks the pattern

The original's mechanism has two halves: prices rise with density, and wages don't keep up. Splitting effort into its numerator and denominator shows which half fails in NZ. Elasticities come from log-log fits on job density.

Australian wages are almost flat across geography, so prices pass straight through into effort. NZ prices respond less to density and wages respond twice as much, so the ratio barely moves. What does predict NZ effort is population growth. Districts that grew fastest in 2013–2023 (Queenstown-Lakes +50%, Selwyn, Waimakariri, Tauranga) are where housing costs the most years of wage. Growth alone gives R² 0.49, or 0.32 without Queenstown, and growth plus density gives 0.53.

This fits the original's own logic, that demand outruns supply, but the right demand measure differs by country. In Europe's large administrative regions, the stock of jobs is a good proxy. In New Zealand's small districts, the flow of new residents (domestic movers, retirees, migrants) predicts better. For Australia, growth over 2020–2023 explains little (R² 0.10) and adds nothing beyond density. That window is short and distorted by the pandemic, so I don't read much into it.

Part six

Tourism

Australia has no regional guest-night series at SA4 level since the ABS accommodation survey ended in 2016, so the tourism proxy is the share of employee jobs in accommodation & food services. NZ has both measures, so I checked the proxy there. Across NZ districts it correlates at 0.89 with commercial guest nights per worker.

Australia reproduces the original's result: density is the main variable, and tourism modulates it. Unlike in Europe, tourism-only fits are not trivial in Australia (R² 0.37), because the coastal amenity belt from Noosa to the Shoalhaven is a large, coherent group rather than a handful of outliers. In NZ neither variable does much on its own. Tourism and lifestyle demand matter in specific places (Queenstown has 141 guest nights per worker against Auckland's 8), but the national pattern is about growth.

Part seven

The mortgage cliff

The original's simulation assumes 90% LTV, 4% interest and 45% of net pay (taken as 70% of gross) going to repayment. Under those assumptions, a buyer whose dwelling costs more than 8.75 years of their income can never repay, because the payment never covers the interest. Measured against one median wage, 78% of Australian jobs and 92% of NZ jobs are in regions past that line for the median dwelling. To keep the numbers informative, the table below uses two median earners buying the median dwelling. That is a plausible household for a family-sized home, and still a generous test.

At 4%, 83% of Australian jobs and 95% of NZ jobs are in regions where even a two-earner household needs more than 15 years. More than half (53% AU, 72% NZ) need more than 25 years. At 6%, a two-earner household on median wages never pays off the median dwelling in regions holding 52% of Australian and 59% of NZ jobs, including all of Sydney, Melbourne, Auckland, Hamilton and Tauranga.

For a rough bridge to the European figures, two Sydney earners buying the median dwelling face the same ratio as one earner on 7.8 years of wage. In the original's terms that would be like a 50 m² flat at 1.9 months of wage per m², above Paris itself before the merge. The dwellings are different goods, so treat this as an order of magnitude rather than a ranking. In practice, buyers bridge the gap with large deposits, family wealth, above-median incomes or long lending terms.

Part eight

Concentration

The original finds every European capital gaining share of national employment since 2000. Australia goes the other way. Greater Sydney's share of employee jobs fell from 21.6% to 20.6% between 2018 and 2023 (ABS Jobs in Australia, by residence). Perth, Brisbane and Adelaide gained share. Sydney is still the most expensive capital, which suggests that recent dispersal has not yet reversed the effect of a high starting density.

New Zealand fits the original's story better. Auckland holds 35% of all employees, more than Lisbon's 25.5% of Portugal. Its share rose from 34.4% in 2016 to 35.2% in 2025. Three districts account for half of national employment, and the employment Gini across districts is 0.70, against 0.46–0.59 in the original's five countries. Growth is fastest in Auckland's satellites and the lifestyle districts: Hamilton metro +27%, Tauranga metro +26%, Queenstown-Lakes +48%, against +16% nationally.

Australian SA4s are designed to hold similar populations for the Labour Force Survey, so a Gini or a “regions to reach 50%” count on them measures the boundary design, not concentration. Those statistics are not reported for Australia.

Part nine

Forty years of effort

The cross-section is a 2023 snapshot. To see how it formed, these series divide the median house price in each capital or metro by average annual earnings of all employees (ABS AWE by state; Stats NZ QES). That denominator is higher than the median employee income used above, so levels sit a little lower than the snapshot. Shaded years splice earnings onto the OECD household disposable-income index, because state AWE starts in 1995 and QES in 1989.

Since 1986, effort has risen 2½ to 3½ times in the big cities of both countries. The OECD's national price-to-income index (disposable income per head) shows the same direction but a smaller rise: 2.2× in Australia and 2.1× in NZ since 1986. Neither series accounts for interest rates. Mortgage rates fell from the mid-to-high teens in the late 1980s to 2–4% in 2021, which is a large part of why prices could rise this far relative to income.

This is the part of the original's argument that the time series can test. The premium compressed from about 2016, accelerated through the pandemic years of remote work, and has not reversed with return-to-office, apart from a slight rebound in Sydney. Prices outside the big cities caught up. That fits the original's mechanism: when people can work at a distance, demand moves out. But it did not make the capitals cheaper relative to wages. It made the rest more expensive.

NZ density is held at 2016 employee counts and pre-2016 industry weights at 2016, since business demography by district starts there. Australian house prices before 2002 are back-cast from the ABS pre-2005 Price Index of Established Homes, so they track quality-adjusted price change rather than the actual median.

Conclusion

What carries over

Holds: in Australia, effort rises with job density and wages do not follow. Coastal amenity regions are the exception, as tourist regions are in Europe. Capital-city effort is extreme. Sydney's median dwelling costs 15.7 years of median wage, and Greater Melbourne, Hobart and Brisbane are at 10.5 to 11.8.

Doesn't hold: in New Zealand, density is not the master variable. Wages keep up with density far better than in Australia, and the high-effort places are the fast-growing ones, whether metropolitan (Auckland, Tauranga) or rural lifestyle (Queenstown, Coromandel, Tasman). The NZ data points to a supply response that lags growth everywhere, not only in dense places.

Over time: effort in the big cities has risen 2½ to 3½ times since 1986. Main-city premiums peaked in the mid-2010s and have compressed since, through pandemic remote work and after it, but the gap closed by the regions rising, not the capitals falling.

The policy reading changes accordingly. In Australia, the original's “feed the corner less” argument has some support, but Sydney is already losing share and is still the least affordable. That points to the stock of housing near existing jobs more than to where new jobs go. In New Zealand, spreading growth around would not help by itself, because growth is what makes districts unaffordable. The binding constraint looks like housing supply's response to growth wherever it lands. That is consistent with NZ's own evidence on land-use regulation, but this analysis does not test it directly.

Method and limitations

  • Prices. AU: median price of established-house and attached-dwelling transfers, year to June 2023, combined as a transfer-weighted average of the two medians. GCCSA medians come directly from ABS. NZ: HUD 12-month rolling median residential sale price to December 2023, from the District Valuation Roll; metro merges use sales-weighted medians.
  • Wages. AU: median employee income from ATO tax data, 2022–23, by residence. NZ: there is no all-industry median by district, so the wage is the employee-weighted average of LEED 2023 median annual earnings by industry, using business-demography employee counts. That is close to a median but not one, and it smooths within-district dispersion.
  • Employment and density. AU: Census 2021 employed persons by place of work. The August 2021 Census fell during the Sydney and Melbourne lockdowns, which distorts SA4 detail, but most of that stays inside the GCCSA merges. NZ: Stats NZ business-demography employees, February 2023 (employment by workplace). Land area: ABS 2021; Stats NZ TA 2020 clipped areas via Wikipedia.
  • Tourism. AU: accommodation & food services share of employee jobs, 2022–23. NZ: commercial guest nights (MBIE Accommodation Data Programme, year to August 2023) per employee; districts with suppressed guest nights are left out of the tourism fits.
  • Fits. OLS of effort on ln(density). Outliers are residuals beyond 1.5×IQR, as in the original. “Density + tourism” is the original's two-predictor model.
  • Not done. No maps, no Part 1 equivalent for Australia's and NZ's own housing-burden surveys, and no per-m² metric, since no public source has floor area for sales. Perth prices have risen sharply since mid-2023, so it would rank higher with current data.
